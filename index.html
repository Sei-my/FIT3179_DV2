THE BIG PICTURE

One continent.
Many ways to get outside.

Seasonal outdoor activities are the focus: start with the yearly calendar, compare summer beaches and winter snow, then examine state-level weather, regions and traveller profiles.

THE TAKEAWAY

There is no single
outdoor Australia.

Australia’s outdoor calendar has more than one rhythm. Beach and surfing trips peak in the summer quarter; snow skiing is concentrated in winter; bushwalking and national-park visits are spread more evenly across the year. State-level temperature and rainfall provide useful context, but they do not establish causation. The clearest lesson is that seasons shape outdoor travel differently across activities and places.

Quarter guide: Q1 = January–March · Q2 = April–June · Q3 = July–September · Q4 = October–December

Data, methods & limitations

Tourism Research Australia

TRA Online, National Visitor Survey (NVS), Domestic Overnight Trips: state-by-quarter activity estimates (2022–2024) and tourism-region activity estimates (2024). Values are estimated overnight trips in thousands (000), not individual activity sessions or unique people. Trips may include multiple activities and stopovers, so activity totals must not be added together.

Bureau of Meteorology

BOM 2024 monthly gridded maximum and minimum temperatures and rainfall were aggregated into approximate state/territory quarterly values using tourism-region boundaries and latitude-based grid-cell area weighting. The mean-temperature measure is derived from maximum and minimum temperatures; quarterly rainfall is an estimated mean depth (mm). These are derived regional estimates, not official BOM state climate summaries.

Geographic alignment

The regional maps use simplified 2026 TRA tourism-region boundaries to display 2024 tourism estimates. Region names were reconciled in the processed data, but boundaries may not perfectly match the 2024 survey geography. The climate tile map is schematic, not a geographically precise map. Climate and travel comparisons are observational associations, not evidence of causation.
